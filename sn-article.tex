\noindent\textbf{Abstract:}\\
The proliferation of satellite megaconstellations in low Earth orbit (LEO) represents a significant advancement in global broadband connectivity. However, we urgently need to understand the potential environmental impacts, particularly greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with these constellations. This study addresses a gap in modeling these emissions by developing an open-source life cycle assessment (LCA) of launch-related CO$_2$ emissions, covering launcher production, transportation, propellant combustion and satellite Kr/Xe propellant production, applied to 12 launch vehicles and 24 megaconstellations. Fully deploying the 24 constellations would emit about 9.1 Mt CO$_2$. Propellant combustion during launch events (60.9\%) and launcher production (31.6\%) together account for 92.5\% of total constellation emissions, while launcher transportation contributes about 1\%. Reusing launch vehicles lowers production emissions per launch by 81--97\% relative to building a new vehicle for every flight, and the number of satellites carried per launch is the assumption with the largest effect on constellation totals. The findings underscore the importance of launch vehicle and satellite design choices to minimize potential environmental impacts. The Open-source Rocket and Constellation Lifecycle Emissions (ORACLE) repository is freely available and aims to facilitate further research in this field. This study provides a critical baseline for policymakers and industry stakeholders to develop strategies for reducing the carbon footprint of the space industry, especially satellite megaconstellations.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} satellite megaconstellations, low Earth orbit (LEO), greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, life cycle assessment (LCA), carbon footprint

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Plain Language Summary:}\\
Satellite constellations are growing rapidly as companies work to provide global internet access. While this expansion offers clear benefits, like connecting remote areas, it also raises concerns about its environmental impact, especially greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from rocket launches and the production of satellite propellant.

This study modeled emissions from 24 satellite networks and 12 different rockets using a new open-source tool. We found that most emissions come from burning rocket propellant and building the rockets, and that reusable rockets like Falcon 9 and Starship cut the production emissions of each launch by over 80\%. How many satellites fit on each launch matters most for a constellation's total emissions. These findings highlight the importance of sustainable design choices as satellite constellations continue to scale.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Climate change poses an existential threat to the planet, with its impacts already evident across society and the economy \citep{huggel_existential_2022, vicedo-cabrera_burden_2021}. Economically, the projected cost of climate change is substantial, with one estimate placing a decline of 11\%–29\% in global income within the next 26 years, irrespective of future emission reductions \citep{kotz_economic_2024}. Extreme weather events exacerbated by climate change impose substantial global costs, on businesses, households and governments, with current estimates at \$143 billion annually, with 63\% of these losses attributed to human mortality \citep{newman_global_2023}. Additionally, climate-induced disruptions could reduce gross domestic product by as much as 10\% on average \citep{waidelich_climate_2024}. These statistics underscore the urgency of adopting ambitious mitigation and adaptation strategies to address climate impacts.

While the environmental impact of human activities on terrestrial ecosystems and the atmosphere has been extensively studied, the sustainability and techno-economics of space activities remains underexplored \citep{osoro_techno-economic_2021}. Satellite megaconstellations, such as SpaceX's Starlink and Amazon's Project Kuiper, hold the potential to revolutionize global communication by providing broadband internet to underserved regions, thereby narrowing the digital divide \citep{del_portillo_technical_2019, giannopapa_space_2022}, especially via implementation in developing countries \citep{wood_charting_2012}. Additionally, space technologies play a crucial role in climate monitoring, disaster response, and Earth observation, offering invaluable data for tracking deforestation, ice sheet melting, and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions \citep{wentz_precise_2000, dinas_innovative_2015}. These benefits demonstrate that space is not inherently detrimental to the environment but rather a domain with both significant opportunities and challenges.

However, as the scale of these systems continues to expand, with proposals for constellations now exceed 100,000 satellites \citep{falle_one_2023}, concerns about their environmental impact and threats to space sustainability have grown. Satellite megaconstellations introduce significant challenges, including increased GHG emissions from frequent rocket launches, risks to atmospheric chemistry from propellant combustion, and the proliferation of space debris in low Earth orbit (LEO). The CO$_2$ emissions from the frequent rocket launches required to deploy and maintain these constellations are projected to grow significantly, amplifying their environmental impact and necessitating urgent scrutiny as launch frequencies rise \citep{dallas_environmental_2020}. These concerns call for a detailed evaluation of the life cycle emissions associated with satellite megaconstellations and the development of strategies to mitigate their environmental footprint \citep{osoro_sustainability_2024}.

This study distinguishes itself by conducting a comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA) of the environmental impact of satellite megaconstellations. Leveraging satellite orbital licensing data, this research quantifies emissions from the production, transportation, and launch of the rockets that deploy current and proposed constellations, together with the production of the satellites' Kr/Xe propellant, considering a range of potential rocket launch vehicles. By aiming to quantify a variety of environmental metrics for these satellite systems, this study provides a detailed understanding of their impact across different lifecycle stages. Furthermore, the findings are presented as an open-source dataset, enabling transparency, reproducibility, and broader applications within the research community. Specifically, the research focuses on three primary questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rocket Emissions: What are the GHG emissions associated with each rocket used as a launch vehicle for satellite deployment?
    \item Constellation Emissions: What are the total emissions generated by each satellite megaconstellation, accounting for production, transportation, and launches?
    \item Per-User Emissions: What are the potential emissions attributable to each user of these satellite services under different adoption scenarios?
\end{enumerate}

By addressing these questions, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the environmental implications of satellite megaconstellations. The analysis aims to inform policymakers, industry stakeholders, and researchers, highlighting opportunities for technological innovation and sustainable practices in the rapidly growing space industry.

The structure of the paper unfolds as follows: Section~\ref{sec2} presents a literature review. In Section~\ref{sec4}, the methodology is outlined, detailing the approach designed to address the stated research questions. Section~\ref{sec5} reports the results, which are subsequently analyzed and discussed in Section~\ref{sec6}. Finally, Section~\ref{sec7} concludes the paper, summarizing key findings and their implications.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec2}

There have been growing concerns around the use of near-Earth space \citep{rossi_future_2024, dambrosio_capacity_2022, sanchez-ortiz_outreach_2025}. The space industry has contributed to unprecedented growth in orbital activity, with the number of active satellites rising to approximately 7,400 by 2023, prompting increased scrutiny of environmental impacts \citep{jones_green_2023}. Lawrence et al. argue that the orbital space surrounding Earth should be regarded as an additional ecosystem, deserving the same level of care and protection as terrestrial ecosystems such as oceans and the atmosphere. They specifically emphasize the concerns for the sustainability of commercial, civic, and military activities in space. This perspective calls for a holistic approach to environmental stewardship that incorporates both terrestrial and orbital ecosystems \citep{lawrence_case_2022}. Recent research further underscores the urgency of managing orbital capacity, demonstrating that while very low Earth orbit (VLEO) could theoretically sustain hundreds of thousands to millions of satellites due to atmospheric drag, higher altitudes face increasing risks of debris accumulation, which could destabilize the orbital environment over centuries \citep{lavezzi_stable_2024}.

LEO satellites play a pivotal role in advancing global communication and navigation technologies, providing service levels unimaginable for remote locations a decade ago \citep{ahmmed_digital_2022, reid_broadband_2018}. Research continues to advance, with recent cellular generations, such as 6G, making non-terrestrial networks (NTNs) a central use case \citep{oughton_reviewing_2024, oughton_future_nodate, shahid_emerging_2024}. There are a range of benefits from LEO networks which increasingly provide high capacity, low latency wide-area coverage, enabling sustainable digital transformation for businesses and improvements in socio-economic opportunities for local communities \citep{liao_integration_2023}. Emerging LEO high-throughput satellite (HTS) constellations, such as OneWeb and Starlink, aim to provide global broadband coverage, with Starlink planning up to 12,000 satellites in two orbital layers to achieve latencies as low as 10–15 ms, making LEO systems integral to the integration of space and terrestrial communication networks \citep{su_broadband_2019}. However, large satellite constellations also pose threats to the sustainability of the orbital environment \citep{bastida_virgili_risk_2016, dambrosio_carrying_2024}.

One of the most pressing environmental challenges stems from the rocket launches required to deploy and maintain large-scale satellite constellations \citep{ross_implications_2022}. Solid rocket propellants release CO$_2$ during combustion, comprising approximately 3.6\% of the total exhaust by mass, alongside other pollutants such as HCl and Al$_2$O$_3$, highlighting the contribution of these launches to atmospheric carbon levels \citep{schuch_rocket_nodate}. Rocket launches in 2019 emitted 5,820 tonnes of CO$_2$, 6,380 tonnes of H$_2$O, 280 tonnes of black carbon (BC), 220 tonnes of nitrogen oxides, 500 tonnes of reactive chlorine, and 910 tonnes of alumina into the stratosphere, highlighting the significant contributions of space activity to atmospheric pollution \citep{brown_worldwide_2024}. Consequently, satellite megaconstellation missions have emerged as a substantial and growing contributor to CO$_2$ emissions from space activities, with their share increasing from 26\% in 2020 to 33\% in 2022 \citep{barker_global_2024}.

While current launch rates are around 100 per year, mega-constellation deployment and maintenance will require dramatic increases. For scale, just the Starlink constellation represents about 3,100 tonnes of material that needs to be launched and replaced on 5-year cycles, resulting in approximately 2 tonnes of material reentering Earth's atmosphere daily.  Modeling suggests that an annual launch cadence of 1,000 hydrocarbon rocket launches could result in radiative forcing levels comparable to those from global subsonic aviation within a decade \citep{boley_satellite_2021}.

Additionally, rocket emissions of BC are up to 500 times more efficient at warming the atmosphere than surface and aviation sources, with sustained launches potentially contributing an additional 7.9 mW $m^{-2}$ to global radiative forcing, doubling the effect of contemporary rockets in just three years \citep{ryan_impact_nodate}. This warming occurs primarily in the stratosphere rather than at the surface, as rocket-emitted BC is injected directly at high altitudes; consequently, it does not offset GHG-driven surface warming but instead introduces distinct vertical radiative and chemical perturbations. Additionally, while this forcing is small relative to total anthropogenic forcing (3 W $m^{-2}$), it is comparable to other niche forcing agents and is disproportionately important due to its direct injection into the stratosphere, where radiative efficiency and impacts on ozone chemistry are significantly enhanced. These BC emissions are predicted to dominate atmospheric impacts by 2050 \citep{tsigaridis_composition_nodate}. The use of solid rocket boosters further introduces damaging particulates, such as alumina, directly into the upper atmosphere and stratosphere, contributing to atmospheric pollution and radiative forcing \citep{ross_radiative_2014}.

The emissions from solid rockets further exacerbate environmental concerns through ozone depletion in the stratosphere \citep{ross_observation_1997, donou-adonsou_space_2024}. Recent modeling indicates that even under ambitious launch growth scenarios, annual-mean near-global total column ozone depletion remains modest, on the order of ~0.3\% (approximately 1 Dobson Unit), though substantially larger regional and seasonal effects are projected, with Antarctic springtime ozone decreases reaching ~3.9\% \citep{revell_near-future_2025}. At similarly elevated launch rates, hydrogen-fueled reusable rockets could increase global stratospheric water vapor by approximately 10\%, enhance polar mesospheric cloud fractions by 20\%, and reduce the globally averaged ozone column by 1.4–1.5 Dobson Units \citep{larson_global_2017}.

The scale of planned satellite megaconstellations also necessitates frequent replenishment launches to replace end-of-life satellites, further increasing the density of objects in LEO. Predictions suggest that an additional 20,000 rocket launches will occur in this decade alone, with between 60,000 and 100,000 satellites in LEO by mid-century \citep{gaston_environmental_nodate}. Unlike traditional large geostationary orbit (GEO) satellites, which are designed to last approximately 20 years, the mean lifespan of modern LEO satellites is significantly shorter, averaging only about 5 years. This shift in satellite lifespan introduces a step change in operational requirements, necessitating continuous replenishment to maintain the functionality of these constellations \citep{chou_towards_2022}. Even modest increases in launch traffic for constellation maintenance are projected to significantly amplify the risk of on-orbit collisions and debris generation \citep{somma_sensitivity_2019, zhang_leo_2022}. Additionally, satellite megaconstellations cause light pollution, negatively impacting the essential human right to dark skies and cultural sky traditions, affecting communities worldwide \citep{venkatesan_impact_2020, varela_perez_increasing_2023}. They also negatively impact telescope observations, potentially threatening the scientific viability of Earth-based telescopes. Simulations suggest that by 2030, 30\% of the images collected by some observatories could contain at least one satellite trail \citep{gwynne_observations_2020}. Thus, despite any albedo-related benefits from increased satellite reflectivity, satellite megaconstellations remain environmentally detrimental overall, as their climate and atmospheric impacts are dominated by launch emissions, stratospheric perturbations, and long-term sustainability risks rather than any meaningful cooling effect \citep{boley_satellite_2021}.

The cumulative environmental effects of rocket launches, ranging from radiative forcing and ozone depletion to orbital crowding and alumina pollution, present significant challenges that must be addressed \citep{brown_envisioning_2024}. In addition, the production of a single Starlink V1 satellite is associated with a total environmental impact of 76 kPt, with resource use of minerals and metals contributing 58 kPt, emphasizing the substantial strain imposed by satellite manufacturing on global mineral resources. Here, kilopoints (kPt) refer to a normalized, dimensionless LCA metric that aggregates multiple environmental impact categories into a single score, normalized relative to the average annual environmental burden of a European citizen, but applicable to global systems and supply chains. \citep{kumaran_quantifying_2024}. Pardini and Anselmo introduced environmental criticality indexes to assess the sustainability of space activities, particularly in LEO. Their findings reveal that between one-third and one-half of LEO's capacity to sustain long-term space activities has already been saturated, underscoring the need for immediate action to manage this finite resource \citep{pardini_environmental_2020, pardini_evaluating_2021}.

Future projections suggest a substantial increase in the environmental impact of the space sector. An LCA by \citet{miraux_environmental_2022-1} estimated that, in a low-growth scenario, the climate change impact of space activities in 2050 would be about nine times its 2021 level, reaching 4.1 Mt CO$_2$e per year, and up to 760 Mt CO$_2$e per year in a high-growth scenario. However, since life cycle impacts must be evaluated holistically rather than focusing solely on individual stages, optimizing vehicle materials and manufacturing methods is essential to achieving meaningful reductions in these emissions over their full operational lifespan \citep{kirchain_jr_environmental_2017}. For instance, harnessing nonlinear orbital perturbation forces can reduce propellant and maintenance costs, potentially decreasing the vehicle mass budget for propellant by approximately 60\% \citep{singh_low_2020}. Additionally, innovative propulsion systems or hybrid rocket propulsion systems could further enhance sustainability \citep{barato_review_2023}.

Initial assessments of emissions have primarily focused on American \citep{harris_space_2019} and European launch vehicles \citep{maury_application_2020}. However, our work highlights the critical need to incorporate a broader range of international launch vehicles into environmental analyses, as this represents a significant step forward in achieving a more comprehensive understanding of the global impact of satellite constellations. As the scope of satellite constellations grows to involve an order-of-magnitude more satellites, this increasing scale underscores the importance of analyzing heavy-lift rockets developed by space-faring nations such as China (Long March) and India (LVM3), which could play significant roles in future deployments.

Each system has a unique emission profile and operational characteristics that must be factored into environmental appraisals.

Satellite megaconstellations offer significant societal benefits, including global broadband connectivity and advancements in communication technologies. However, their rapid growth also presents profound challenges to environmental and space sustainability. These challenges demand a balanced and holistic approach to environmental stewardship that extends to the orbital domain. 

Efforts to quantify the long-term impacts and promote sustainable practices are essential. Advancing reusability in launch vehicles, optimizing satellite lifecycles, and incorporating eco-friendly materials and propellants represent promising pathways forward. As the space sector evolves, maintaining a balance between innovation and sustainability will be key to its long-term viability.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec4}

For the purposes of this analysis, constellations with at least 150 planned satellites were categorized as megaconstellations. All such constellations with International Telecommunication Union (ITU) filings are included in the emissions estimates presented here \citep{itu_e-submission_nodate}, with the exception of the SpinLaunch constellation. Unlike traditional systems, SpinLaunch utilizes a kinetic launch mechanism rather than rockets \citep{noauthor_spinlaunch_nodate}, and due to the lack of publicly available data on its emissions, it was excluded from this study.

Our methodology involves conducting a comprehensive LCA of emissions associated with satellite megaconstellations. The system boundary covers launch vehicle production (cradle-to-gate structural materials and electronics), transportation of launch vehicles to the launch site, propellant combustion at launch, and production of the satellites' Kr/Xe propellant. It excludes satellite manufacturing, electricity for fabricating and assembling launch vehicles (for which no vehicle-specific data are available; electricity used to produce the materials is included in the cradle-to-gate material factors), upstream propellant production, non-CO$_2$ combustion products, and altitude-dependent atmospheric effects such as stratospheric radiative forcing and ozone chemistry. All results are reported as CO$_2$-equivalent (CO$_2$e) mass using the emission factors cited for each stage, without altitude-specific adjustment. This includes detailed calculations of emissions from multiple stages of rocket launches and satellite operations, while incorporating the effects of reusability. The stages considered in our analysis encompass:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Propellant Combustion} – Emissions released during rocket launches.
    \item \textbf{Rocket Launcher Production} – Environmental costs of manufacturing launch vehicles.
    \item \textbf{Transportation} – Emissions from logistics and transportation of launch vehicles and components.
    \item \textbf{Satellite Propellant Production} – Emissions from producing the krypton and xenon propellant carried by each satellite's electric propulsion system. Manufacturing of the satellites themselves is not included.
\end{enumerate}

In addition to estimating the overall emissions from these stages, we assess their environmental impact on a per-subscriber basis under various scenarios, providing a perspective on the emissions intensity relative to the service provided.

The following equations form the foundation of our emission analysis, providing a mathematical framework for quantifying and comparing emissions across different constellations.

\subsection{Total Emissions from Propellant Combustion}\label{sec:propellant}

Propellant combustion is the primary source of emissions in rocket launches. The total emissions from propellant combustion \( E_{\text{propellant}} \) are calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{propellant}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( M_{i} \times EF_{i} \right)
\end{equation}

The term \( M_{i} \) denotes the mass of the \( i^{\text{th}} \) propellant type, while \( EF_{i} \) is the corresponding emission factor, indicating the amount of emissions per unit mass of propellant. The summation is performed across all \( N \) propellant types used in the analysis.

In this study, the analysis includes five common propellant types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Solid} – Ammonium perchlorate (NH\(_4\)ClO\(_4\)), aluminum (Al), and hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene (HTPB),
    \item \textbf{Cryogenic (hydrolox)} – Liquid oxygen (LO\(_x\)) and liquid hydrogen (LH\(_2\)), whose combustion product is water vapor (H\(_2\)O),
    \item \textbf{Kerosene-based} – Liquid oxygen (LO\(_x\)) and Refined Petroleum-1 (RP-1),
    \item \textbf{Methane-based (methalox)} – Liquid oxygen (LO\(_x\)) and liquid methane (CH\(_4\)), used on Starship, New Glenn's first stage and Vulcan Centaur's first stage,
    \item \textbf{Hypergolic} – Nitrogen tetroxide (N\(_2\)O\(_4\)) and unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH).
\end{itemize}

Each emission factor is the mass of CO$_2$ formed per kilogram of propellant when all carbon in the propellant is oxidized to CO$_2$, either in the combustion chamber or by afterburning of CO in the exhaust plume:

\begin{equation}
EF_{i} = f_{i} \times c_{i} \times \frac{44.009}{12.011}
\end{equation}

Here \( f_{i} \) is the mass fraction of the carbon-bearing component in propellant \( i \), \( c_{i} \) is the carbon mass fraction of that component, and \( 44.009/12.011 \) converts a mass of carbon to the mass of CO$_2$ it forms. For bipropellants, \( f_{i} = 1/(1 + O/F) \), where \( O/F \) is the oxidizer-to-fuel mass ratio. Table~\ref{tab:propellant_factors} lists the assumed compositions and the resulting factors. Because complete oxidation is assumed, these are upper bounds on direct CO$_2$ per unit of propellant. Since most modern rockets utilize a combination of these propellants, Equation 1 sums the contribution of each type.

The zero emission factor for cryogenic (hydrolox) propellant reflects zero direct CO$_2$ only; it excludes the radiative effect of water vapor emitted in the upper atmosphere and emissions from hydrogen production.

\subsection{Total Production Emissions}

The production of rockets contributes significantly to emissions, arising from the materials and electronics used in their construction. The total emissions from rocket production are calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{production}} = DM_{j} \times EF_{j} + DM_{e} \times EF_{e}
\end{equation}
\\
In this equation, \( E_{\text{production}} \) represents the total emissions from rocket production. The term \( DM_{j} \) denotes the dry mass of structural material \( j \), taken as 95\% of the launcher's dry mass, with \( EF_{j} \) as the corresponding cradle-to-gate emission factor for material \( j \). Similarly, \( DM_{e} \) refers to the dry mass of the electronics components, and \( EF_{e} \) is the emission factor associated with electronics manufacturing.

For this analysis, all rockets except SpaceX's Starship are assumed to be constructed using an aluminum-lithium alloy, which consists of approximately 97\% aluminum and 3\% lithium. Starship, on the other hand, is expected to use stainless steel, and the respective emission factors for these materials have been applied. These factors were sourced from reliable material databases to ensure precision in calculations \citep{iai_notitle_nodate}.

Additionally, the dry mass of the electronics is estimated to be approximately 5\% of the total dry mass of the rocket. This figure is based on historical data from NASA vehicle designs and is used consistently across the analysis to estimate the electronics-related emissions \citep{motiwala_conceptual_2014}. This category encompasses various electronic components essential for the functionality and control of the rocket, including avionics systems, flight computers, sensors, communication systems, power distribution units, telemetry systems, and guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) hardware. By accounting for both the structural materials and the electronics, this method provides a comprehensive estimate of the production emissions for each rocket design.

It is important to note that all rocket and satellite manufacturing is assumed to occur at the Earth’s surface. Metal particulates generated during production are therefore expected to deposit locally on short timescales and are not treated as atmospheric climate forcers in this study. Their climate relevance arises indirectly through energy use and process-related GHG emissions, which are captured through the cradle-to-gate emission factors applied in the production term.

\subsection{Adjusted Production Emissions for Reusable Rockets}\label{sec:reuse}

Reusable rockets have a distinct emissions profile due to their ability to reduce the need for new production for each subsequent launch. The adjusted production emissions per launch for reusable rockets are calculated as:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{reusable}} = E_{\text{production}} \times A
\end{equation}
\\
In this equation, \( E_{\text{reusable}} \) denotes the adjusted emissions per launch, and \( A \), the amortization factor, is the share of a full vehicle's production emissions charged to each launch. The amortization factor is defined as:

\begin{equation}
A = \left( 1 - S1P \right) + \frac{S1P}{R} + R_A \times \frac{R - 1}{R}
\end{equation}
\\
Here, \( S1P \) represents the proportion of the rocket's mass attributed to the reusable first stage, and \( R \) is the number of flights each reusable stage completes over its life. The first term charges every launch for the expendable stages, which are discarded and rebuilt for each flight. The second term spreads the production of the reusable stage over its \( R \) flights. The third term adds refurbishment: each of the \( R - 1 \) reflights is charged a refurbishment cost \( R_A \), expressed as a fraction of full-vehicle production emissions, assumed to be 0.02 (2\%) for all reusable rockets, including any emissions from transportation to refurbishment facilities. Equivalently, the first flight of a vehicle is charged the full vehicle, and each reflight is charged \( (1 - S1P) + R_A \); \( A \) is the average over the \( R \) flights. Expendable rockets have \( A = 1 \).

This equation is applied to partially reusable rockets such as Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, and New Glenn, where only Stage 1 is reused. For fully reusable rockets like SpaceX's Starship, the entire vehicle is reused, so \( S1P = 1 \) and the amortization factor reduces to:

\begin{equation}
A = \frac{1}{R} + R_A \times \frac{R - 1}{R}
\end{equation}
\\
Because no stage is discarded, fully reusable systems offer a greater potential for emissions reduction. By incorporating these calculations, the model captures the environmental benefits of reusability while enabling a fair comparison between reusable and expendable rockets. Thus, this factor allows for production emissions to be distributed over the lifetime of the asset.

\subsection{Transportation Emissions}

The transportation of rocket components to launch sites contributes significantly to the total emissions associated with rocket launches. These emissions are calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{transport}} = \sum_{v=1}^{V} \left( M_{v} \times D_{v} \times EF_{v} \right)
\end{equation}
\\
In this equation, \( E_{\text{transport}} \) represents the total emissions from transportation. The term \( M_{v} \) denotes the mass transported by vehicle type \( v \) (in tonnes, taken as the launcher's dry mass), \( D_{v} \) is the distance traveled by that vehicle type (in km), and \( EF_{v} \) is the emission factor for the corresponding vehicle type (in kg CO$_2$e per tonne-km). Launch vehicles are shipped without propellant, which is produced or delivered at or near the launch site; delivery of propellant is not included because vehicle-specific supply chain data are not available. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} reports the effect of also counting the propellant mass, as an upper-bound proxy for propellant delivery. The summation is performed across \( V \), the total number of distinct vehicle types involved in transportation.

Emission factors were considered for four primary vehicle types used in transporting rocket components: trucks, trains, cargo aircraft, and container ships \citep{cbo_emissions_nodate}. Notably, for the Ariane 6 rocket, the emission factor for container ships was adjusted to account for the use of the \textit{Canopée}, an eco-friendly container ship equipped with sails that reduce emissions by approximately 30\% \citep{werner_orcelle_2023}. The \textit{Canopée}, in operation since 2022, reflects the unique efforts made to minimize the environmental impact of transportation for specific rockets.

The distance traveled by each vehicle type was calculated as the shortest possible route between the rocket’s manufacturing site and its launch site. For ground transport, distances were determined using Google Maps, while air and sea routes were estimated using Google Earth. This method ensures consistency and accuracy in estimating transportation-related emissions. Some uncertainty arises from variability in routing, vehicle utilization, and logistical assumptions (e.g., multimodal transport and partial loads); these uncertainties are not quantified in this study.

\subsection{Launch Emissions Per Rocket}

The total emissions for each rocket launch, encompassing adjusted production emissions, propellant combustion, and transportation emissions, are calculated as:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{launch\_per\_rocket}} = E_{\text{reusable}} + E_{\text{propellant}} + E_{\text{transport}}
\end{equation}
\\
This equation provides a comprehensive measure of the emissions associated with a single rocket launch, combining the key contributors to its environmental impact. This enables direct comparisons between different launch vehicles by providing a standardized measure of their total environmental impact per launch.

\subsection{Total Launch Emissions for Satellite Constellations}\label{sec:launches}

The total emissions from launches required to deploy satellite constellations are given by:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{launch}} = \sum_{m=1}^{K} \left( \left\lceil \frac{N_{\text{satellite},m}}{N_{\text{per\_rocket},m}} \right\rceil \times E_{\text{launch\_per\_rocket},m} \right)
\end{equation}
\\
In this equation, \( E_{\text{launch}} \) represents the total emissions from all launches across the constellations analyzed. The variable \( N_{\text{satellite},m} \) denotes the total number of satellites in constellation \( m \), while \( N_{\text{per\_rocket},m} \) is the number of satellites carried per rocket for that constellation. The number of launches is rounded up to a whole launch, \( E_{\text{launch\_per\_rocket},m} \) is the per-launch emission of the rocket modeled for constellation \( m \), and the summation is performed across \( K \), the total number of constellations included in the analysis.

To conduct this LCA, certain assumptions were necessary to address gaps in publicly available data. For constellations that utilize multiple rocket types, the most frequently used rocket was selected for emissions calculations to ensure consistency. In cases where no information was available on the number of satellites per rocket, this value was assumed to be 10, based on typical payload capacities. For constellations whose planned launcher is not among the modeled vehicles, a modeled rocket is used in its place: Long March 5 for Kuaizhou-1A (KLEO), Zhuque-2 (Honghu-3) and South Korea's Nuri (Hanwha), and Falcon 9 for Rocket Lab's Electron (Astra, Cinnamon-937 and Semaphore-C). In these cases the planned number of satellites per launch is scaled by the ratio of the payload capacities of the modeled and planned rockets and rounded down, so that each modeled launch carries the number of satellites its payload capacity allows rather than the number a much smaller rocket would carry. For every constellation, the number of satellites per launch is also capped at the modeled rocket's payload capacity divided by the satellite mass. Each constellation is classified as using a reusable or an expendable launcher according to the rocket whose emissions are charged to it.

This approach enables a standardized approach to emissions estimation across a diverse range of constellations while ensuring methodological consistency and transparency. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} reports how the results change if the planned number of satellites per launch is used without scaling.

\subsection{Total Emissions from Satellite Propellant Production}

Each satellite is assumed to carry krypton or xenon propellant for its electric (ion) propulsion system, and the production of these gases contributes to overall emissions. Manufacturing of the satellites themselves (structure, payload, electronics, solar arrays and assembly) is outside the scope of this study. The total emissions from Kr/Xe propellant production are calculated as:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{Kr/Xe}} = \sum_{n=1}^{S} \left( M_{\text{sat},n} \times \left( X_{\text{kx}} \times E_{\text{kx}} \right) \right)
\end{equation}

In this equation, \( E_{\text{Kr/Xe}} \) represents the total emissions resulting from the production of the satellites' Kr/Xe propellant. The term \( M_{\text{sat},n} \) denotes the mass of satellite \( n \), while \( X_{\text{kx}} \) is the proportion of krypton or xenon within the satellite mass. For the purposes of this analysis, \( X_{\text{kx}} \) is assumed to be a constant value of 2\% across all satellites. The variable \( E_{\text{kx}} \) represents the emission factor for the production of krypton and xenon, reflecting the environmental impact of their manufacturing processes. The summation is performed across all satellites in the constellation, as indicated by \( S \).

This approach provides a standardized estimate of emissions from Kr/Xe propellant production across constellations. The constant proportion of krypton and xenon provides a consistent basis for comparison. We found no published emission factor for producing krypton or xenon from air; \( E_{\text{kx}} \) is therefore an assumed value of 546~kg CO$_2$e per kg, reflecting the high electricity demand of separating these trace gases, and Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} reports the effect of halving or doubling it. The 2\% propellant fraction is applied to every satellite, including small satellites that may carry no electric propulsion.

\subsection{Number of Subscribers}\label{sec:subscribers}

The number of subscribers for each constellation is calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
N_{\text{subscriber}} = N_{\text{satellite}} \times S_{\text{factor}}
\end{equation}
\\
In this equation, \( N_{\text{subscriber}} \) represents the number of subscribers served by the constellation. The variable \( N_{\text{satellite}} \) is the total number of satellites in the constellation, while \( S_{\text{factor}} \) denotes the subscriber factor, defined as the average number of subscribers supported per satellite (489.5).

The subscriber factor \( S_{\text{factor}} \) was determined using publicly available data from the Starlink constellation \citep{ma_network_2023}, as it is the only megaconstellation analyzed that has released information on its current number of subscribers. For other constellations, where subscriber data is not available, \( S_{\text{factor}} \) was assumed to be similar to that of Starlink, regardless of the total number of planned satellites. While this assumption ensures consistency across the analysis, actual subscribers per satellite are likely to differ among constellations because of differences in operational goals, target markets, and technology. The Monte Carlo analysis in Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty} applies a 10\% standard deviation to each constellation's subscriber estimate; it does not capture systematic differences in the subscriber factor between constellations, which may be larger. Some constellations in the set do not provide broadband to subscribers: Flock is an Earth-observation constellation, and Swarm, Lacuna and Lynk provide Internet-of-Things or direct-to-device services. For these, emissions per subscriber should be read as emissions per broadband-equivalent user under the Starlink subscriber factor, not as a measured service intensity.

\subsection{Per Subscriber Emissions}

The emissions attributable to each subscriber are calculated using the equation:

\begin{equation}
E_{\text{subscriber}} = \frac{E_{\text{launch}} + E_{\text{Kr/Xe}}}{N_{\text{subscriber}}}
\end{equation}

In this equation, \( E_{\text{subscriber}} \) represents the emissions per subscriber. The numerator combines the total emissions from rocket launches, \( E_{\text{launch}} \), and Kr/Xe propellant production, \( E_{\text{Kr/Xe}} \), capturing the overall environmental impact of deploying and maintaining the satellite constellation. The denominator, \( N_{\text{subscriber}} \), represents the number of subscribers served by the constellation, as determined in the previous section.

It is also important to note that competition among constellations for a limited subscriber market could lead to fewer subscribers per satellite than projected. This would further increase the emissions per subscriber, highlighting the environmental risks associated with market overestimation. Additionally, electricity consumption from user terminals and ground antennas is not included due to limited global usage data; inclusion of this term would increase per-subscriber emissions estimates and thus render our results conservative.

This formulation provides a per-subscriber metric that reflects the environmental cost of satellite-based communication networks. By incorporating both launch and production emissions, the calculation ensures a comprehensive assessment of the emissions intensity associated with satellite services.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification}\label{sec:uncertainty}
The uncertainty analysis addresses a single input: the number of subscribers, which rests on the assumption that every constellation achieves Starlink's subscribers per satellite (Section~\ref{sec:subscribers}). For each constellation, the number of subscribers is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean equal to the estimate from Equation 11 and standard deviation equal to \( 0.10 \times \) that mean; non-positive draws are rejected and redrawn. All other inputs (propellant masses and emission factors, production, electricity, transportation, reusability and satellite propellant parameters) are held at their deterministic values, so total emissions for each constellation are fixed and only the denominator of Equation 12 varies.

The simulation runs 10,000 iterations with a fixed random seed. In each iteration, emissions per subscriber are computed for every constellation, for the mean across constellations, and for each country (total emissions divided by total subscribers of that country's constellations). We report the deterministic estimate together with the standard deviation of the simulated values, written as estimate (\( \pm \) 1 SD). The 10\% input standard deviation is not itself an interval on the results. Because emissions per subscriber vary inversely with the number of subscribers, the output standard deviation for an individual constellation is slightly larger than 10\% of its estimate (10.2--10.5\%). For countries with several constellations and for the mean across constellations it is smaller, because independent draws for several constellations partly offset one another (about 7\% for China, Russia and the United States); for example, the mean across constellations is 286 (\( \pm \) 9) kg, a standard deviation of about 3\%. These intervals therefore reflect uncertainty in subscriber numbers alone and understate the total uncertainty of the results, since uncertainty in launch, production, transportation and satellite propellant emissions is not quantified.

\subsection{Limitations}
This analysis quantifies GHG emissions using an LCA framework and does not explicitly resolve the vertical distribution of emissions or altitude-dependent chemical and removal processes. While rocket launches inject gases and particulates across the troposphere, stratosphere, and mesosphere, emissions are aggregated as CO$_2$-equivalent mass in accordance with standard LCA practice. Non-CO$_2$ species such as BC, alumina, and reactive halogens, as well as altitude-specific processes including sedimentation, deposition, and photochemical destruction, are therefore not explicitly modeled. The results should be interpreted as a climate-relevant emissions inventory rather than a detailed atmospheric chemistry assessment. Future work should couple life-cycle emissions estimates with chemistry–climate models to better capture altitude-dependent impacts.

Several further simplifications bound the scope of the results. Propellant emission factors assume complete oxidation of carbon to CO$_2$ and are therefore upper bounds on direct CO$_2$. Payload capacities are each rocket's maximum to LEO, which for reusable rockets is the expendable-mode value; with booster recovery, fewer satellites fit on each launch. Satellites per launch follow operator plans and ITU filings, and the Long March 5 stands in for all Chinese and South Korean launchers. Satellite manufacturing, launcher fabrication electricity, upstream propellant production and ground segment electricity are excluded, and the Kr/Xe emission factor is an assumed value. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} quantifies the effect of the main assumptions.

\subsection{Methodology Overview and Code Accessibility}

The equations and parameters outlined in this study enable a systematic analysis of the environmental impact of satellite megaconstellations, offering critical insights into their sustainability and emissions profiles. This framework facilitates a comprehensive LCA that accounts for production, transportation, and launch phases.

The code used for this analysis is part of the Open-source Rocket and Constellation Lifecycle Emissions (ORACLE) project \citep{kukreja_open-source_nodate}. The repository includes the Python model that computes every result, the Monte Carlo and sensitivity analyses, visualization scripts developed in R, and the input data required to replicate and expand upon the results presented in this study. By making this code accessible, the ORACLE project encourages transparency and fosters collaboration in the study of space sustainability.

\section{Results}\label{sec5}

The results of our study provide a comprehensive overview of the GHG emissions associated with satellite megaconstellations, focusing on different stages of rocket launches and satellite operations. 

\subsection{Emissions Per Rocket}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{d_rocket_emissions.png}
    \caption{Total and adjusted GHG emissions for each launch vehicle.}
    \label{fig:rocket_emissions}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:rocket_emissions} provides a comprehensive analysis of GHG emissions associated with various launch vehicles, highlighting total emissions, payload-adjusted emissions, and the effects of reusability on their environmental impact.

The average total emissions for a rocket are approximately 1.23 kt per launch, equivalent to the annual carbon absorption of 56,000 trees \citep{encon_calculation_nodate}. The breakdown of these emissions is as follows: 608 t from propellant combustion (49.3\%), 566 t from launcher production (45.9\%), 50 t from electronics production (4.1\%), and 9 t from transportation (0.7\%).

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:rocket_emissions}A, the Starship rocket (with a payload of 125 t) exhibits the highest emissions, producing approximately 2.83 kt of CO$_2$ per launch, with 97\% of these emissions stemming from propellant combustion during the launch event. In contrast, Falcon 9 (with a payload of 22.8 t) has the lowest emissions, at 527 t per launch. Across all rockets, propellant combustion (49.3\%) and launcher production (45.9\%) together account for 95.2\% of emissions per launch, while electronics production (4.1\%) and transportation (0.7\%) play smaller roles.

When emissions are adjusted to account for payload masses, the relative efficiency of the rockets (pictured in Figure \ref{fig:rocket_emissions}B) shifts significantly. LVM3 stands out with the highest adjusted emissions, reaching 154 t of CO$_2$ per tonne of payload capacity, well above the average of approximately 60 t. New Glenn has the lowest adjusted emissions, at 21.9 t per tonne of payload. This adjustment highlights the importance of payload capacity as a critical factor in evaluating the environmental impact of launch systems.

Reusable rockets, such as Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, and New Glenn, demonstrate substantially lower production emissions per launch, as evident from Figure \ref{fig:rocket_emissions}D. Under the reuse assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:reuse}, spreading production over the life of the reusable stages lowers production emissions per launch by 82\% for Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy, 81\% for New Glenn and 97\% for the fully reusable Starship, relative to building a new vehicle for every flight. Production accounts for 14.1\%, 12.4\% and 27.8\% of the per-launch emissions of Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy and New Glenn, respectively, compared to an average of 45.9\% across all analyzed launch vehicles. These reusable rockets also achieve the lowest emissions per tonne of payload capacity, all below 23,100~kg of CO$_2$ per tonne. Launcher transportation contributes less than 3\% of per-launch emissions for every rocket, and less than 1\% on average.

The impact of reusability on emissions is further highlighted in comparisons between initial and subsequent launches in Figure \ref{fig:rocket_emissions}C. For reusable rockets, subsequent launches result in significantly reduced emissions. For instance, Falcon 9's emissions decrease from 900 t on a launch with a newly built vehicle to 523 t on a launch with a reflown first stage, which still requires a new upper stage.

\subsection{Emissions Per Satellite Constellation}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{e_constellation_emissions.png}
    \caption{Total and adjusted GHG emissions for each satellite megaconstellation \& emissions for megaconstellations by country and by rocket type.}
    \label{fig:constellation_emissions}
\end{figure}

The GHG emissions associated with satellite megaconstellations reveal significant variability across deployment strategies and constellation sizes. Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_emissions} illustrates the total emissions from rocket launches, production of satellite Kr/Xe propellant, and other associated processes.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_emissions}A, Starlink (Gen2) exhibits the highest total emissions, at 1,970 kt, while the Flock constellation records the lowest at approximately 2 kt. Fully deploying all 24 constellations requires about 9,100 launches and emits 9.1~Mt CO$_2$e. These disparities highlight the dominant role of larger constellations in driving cumulative environmental impacts.

Across all constellations, the majority of emissions (92.5\%) originate from two sources: the launch event (60.9\%) and launcher production (31.6\%). Satellite Kr/Xe propellant production (3.5\%), electronics production (2.8\%) and launcher transportation (1.1\%) contribute comparatively minor fractions to the total footprint. For instance, over 90\% of Starlink's emissions are attributed to launch events, reflecting the propellant combustion of its fully reusable launcher. Conversely, constellations launched on expendable rockets show a much higher contribution from launcher production, which accounts for 55.1\% of Lacuna's emissions and 63.9\% of Xingshidai's.

Emissions per rocket launch, displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_emissions}B, also demonstrate notable differences across constellations. Starlink records the highest emissions per launch at 2,999 t of CO$_2$, followed by Hanwha at 1,971 t and KLEO at 1,843 t, both modeled on the expendable Long March 5. Sixteen of the 24 constellations have emissions below 1,500 t per launch. Emissions from producing satellite Kr/Xe propellant are significant for constellations of comparatively heavy satellites on reusable launchers, accounting for 31.9\% of Astra's total and 20.8\% of Kuiper's; this share depends directly on the assumed Kr/Xe emission factor (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

When emissions are analyzed by impact category, both in Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_emissions}A and Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_emissions}B, considerable variability emerges. The launch event contributes 91.5\% of Starlink's emissions but only 25.8\% of Hanwha's. Similarly, production-related emissions range from 2.3\% of total emissions for Starlink to 63.9\% for Xingshidai, reflecting differences in launch vehicle choice. Constellations launched on expendable rockets experience a disproportionate impact from production-related emissions, because every launch requires a newly built vehicle.

The influence of rocket reusability is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_emissions}D. For the 13 constellations modeled on reusable rockets, launcher production contributes 9.3\% of total emissions and the launch event 83.5\%. For the 11 constellations on expendable rockets, launcher production contributes 61.7\% and the launch event 30.5\%. Average total emissions are similar for the two groups (404 kt and 353 kt), because the reusable group contains the largest constellation, Starlink; differences in constellation size and satellite mass therefore outweigh reusability in total emissions, while reusability determines how those emissions divide between production and propellant combustion.

\subsection{Emissions Per User}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{f_per_subscriber_emissions.png}
    \caption{GHG emissions per subscriber, by satellite megaconstellation and by country. Error bars show $\pm$1 standard deviation from the Monte Carlo analysis of subscriber numbers (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}).}
    \label{fig:subscriber_emissions}
\end{figure}

We also undertake a scenario analysis of emissions on a per-user basis for each constellation, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:subscriber_emissions}. Since the number of modeled subscribers varies greatly, ranging from 73,425 for Swarm and Flock to 165,119,609 for Cinnamon-937, it is important to adjust for this factor. Most constellations, except for Cinnamon-937, Semaphore-C, and Starlink, serve fewer than 7 million users.

The mean of emissions per user across constellations is 286 ($\pm$ 9) kg (estimate $\pm$ 1 SD from the Monte Carlo analysis of subscriber numbers; Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}). Dividing the total emissions of all constellations by their total subscribers gives a much lower 34 kg, because the largest constellations, which have the most subscribers, have low emissions per subscriber. Figure~\ref{fig:subscriber_emissions}A shows that Xingwang has the highest emissions per subscriber, at 1,163 ($\pm$ 121) kg of CO$_2$, followed by Globalstar at 1,091 ($\pm$ 114) kg; both carry few satellites per launch relative to their launchers' capacity. Starlink's emissions per subscriber are 102 ($\pm$ 10) kg. Cinnamon-937 and Semaphore-C have the lowest emissions per subscriber, at 4 kg each, because each 10~kg satellite is assigned the same number of subscribers as a Starlink satellite (Section~\ref{sec:subscribers}); these values should not be read as evidence of efficient service provision.

The emissions trends extend to a comparison of per-subscriber emissions across countries in Figure~\ref{fig:subscriber_emissions}B, computed as each country's total emissions divided by its total subscribers. Russia records the highest emissions per subscriber, at 429 ($\pm$ 32) kg of CO$_2$, 49.9\% above the mean across constellations, driven by the Rassvet and SferaCon constellations, which are modeled on the expendable Soyuz-FG. China follows at 270 ($\pm$ 18) kg and the United States at 137 ($\pm$ 9) kg. The United Kingdom and Canada are lower, at 89 ($\pm$ 9) kg and 78 ($\pm$ 8) kg. These differences reflect the launch vehicles and satellites per launch assumed for each constellation rather than measured national practices.

\subsection{Contextualizing Emissions Relative to Other GHG Sources}
To contextualize the magnitude of these emissions, it is instructive to compare the environmental footprint of satellite megaconstellations with other well-established sources of GHG emissions. Our analysis reveals that the average emissions per rocket launch are approximately 1.23 kt CO$_2$, which is comparable to the annual emissions of approximately 270 passenger vehicles \citep{epa_greenhouse_2015} or the carbon footprint of 134 U.S. households for one year \citep{eia_frequently_nodate}. When scaled across entire constellations, the emissions become substantially more significant. For instance, the complete deployment of the Starlink Gen 2 constellation, with 39,396 satellites and estimated total emissions of 1,970 kt CO$_2$, produces GHG emissions equivalent to approximately 428,000 passenger vehicles annually.

On a per-subscriber basis, the mean of 286 kg CO$_2$ per user corresponds, when spread over the 5-year satellite replacement cycle assumed in this study, to approximately 57 kg CO$_2$ per user per year. This represents approximately 1.1\% of the average annual carbon footprint of a U.S. citizen (approximately 5,000 kg CO$_2$ per year) \citep{ritchie_co2_nodate} or about 2.9\% of the global per capita average (approximately 2,000 kg CO$_2$ per year) \citep{friedlingstein_global_nodate}. In comparison with terrestrial broadband infrastructure, this annualized figure is roughly 1--3 times that of fiber-optic networks, which typically generate 20--50 kg CO$_2$ per subscriber annually, and within the 50--150 kg CO$_2$ per subscriber range reported for 5G networks \citep{malmodin_energy_2018}. These per-user figures exclude satellite manufacturing and user terminals and are sensitive to the assumed subscribers per satellite (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).

When compared to the broader space industry, satellite megaconstellations represent a growing fraction of total space-sector emissions. Current estimates suggest that the global space industry generates approximately 1--5 Mt CO$_2$ annually, with launch activities accounting for roughly 60--70\% of this total \citep{barker_global_2024}. Fully deploying the 24 constellations analyzed would emit approximately 9.1 Mt CO$_2$, or about 1.8 Mt CO$_2$ per year if spread over the 5-year satellite replacement cycle, comparable to the current emissions of the entire space sector. Relative to global anthropogenic emissions of approximately 37,000 Mt CO$_2$ per year \citep{friedlingstein_global_nodate}, this corresponds to roughly 0.005\% of total annual emissions. However, this small fraction masks two concerns: first, launch cadences are projected to rise from approximately 100 launches per year in 2020 to potentially 1,000 or more launches annually \citep{boley_satellite_2021}; and second, emissions injected directly into the stratosphere have disproportionate effects on radiative forcing and ozone chemistry compared to surface-level emissions \citep{ryan_impact_nodate, tsigaridis_composition_nodate}, effects that a CO$_2$ inventory does not capture. While satellite constellation emissions remain modest relative to major sectors such as global aviation (approximately 1,000 Mt CO$_2$ annually) \citep{lee_contribution_2021} or maritime shipping (approximately 1,100 Mt CO$_2$ annually) \citep{imo_fourth_nodate}, launch cadences approaching 10$^3$ launches per year could drive space-sector climate impacts toward aviation-comparable radiative forcing levels \citep{boley_satellite_2021}.

\subsection{Comparison of Results}
Brown et al. quantified an average of 146 t of emissions deposited in the stratosphere (15--50 km) per launch \citep{brown_worldwide_2024}, whereas our analysis estimates an average of 608 t of CO$_2$ from propellant combustion per launch. The two figures are not directly comparable: Brown et al. model the altitude-resolved deposition of all exhaust species for the launches flown in a given year, while we estimate total CO$_2$ at all altitudes for a fixed set of 12 vehicles under complete oxidation. Both studies nonetheless identify propellant combustion as a major source of launch-related emissions; in our inventory it is the largest single stage, at 60.9\% of total constellation emissions.

Both studies highlight large heterogeneity in per-launch emissions across the global rocket fleet, with vehicle-specific impacts differing by more than a factor of five in our analysis, indicating that average launch metrics obscure large vehicle-specific differences. Both also show that environmental impacts scale approximately linearly with launch cadence, implying that a shift from historical launch rates on the order of 10$^2$ launches per year to projected megaconstellation deployment scenarios approaching 10$^3$ launches per year represents a clear step change relative to past spaceflight activity \citep{brown_worldwide_2024}. Broader quantitative comparison is limited because few other existing studies assess rocket emissions at a comparable global scale and with equivalent lifecycle coverage.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec6}

Our analysis of GHG emissions associated with satellite megaconstellations provides insights into the drivers of emissions at multiple scales (individual rockets, entire constellations, and on a per subscriber basis), addressing the three research questions outlined in the Introduction.

The launch event and launcher production emerged as the dominant contributors, collectively accounting for 92.5\% of total emissions (60.9\% and 31.6\%, respectively), while launcher transportation contributes about 1\%. Reducing these emissions therefore depends mainly on propellant choice, vehicle reuse and filling each launch closer to its payload capacity. The sensitivity analysis shows that the number of satellites carried per launch is the most influential assumption, which makes launch packing efficiency a direct lever on constellation emissions.

Reusable rockets, such as SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Starship, represent a promising pathway for emissions reduction: under our reuse assumptions, they lower production emissions per launch by 81--97\% relative to building a new vehicle for every flight, and this result changes little if each reusable stage flies 10 or 20 times instead of 100. However, Starship's large size results in the highest total emissions per launch, at 2.83 kt CO$_2$. Nonetheless, its emissions per tonne of payload capacity are relatively low at 22.6 t CO$_2$, demonstrating its efficiency for large-scale deployments. Because propellant combustion dominates the per-launch emissions of reusable rockets, lower-carbon propellants such as methane, or hydrogen produced with low-carbon energy, are the main remaining lever for these vehicles.

The variability in per-subscriber emissions across constellations introduces an important dimension of environmental efficiency. Under our assumptions, Xingwang and Globalstar produce over 1,000 kg of CO$_2$ per subscriber, about four times the mean, because they carry few satellites per launch relative to their launchers' capacity. In contrast, Starlink produces 102 kg per subscriber. These findings highlight the importance of filling launches and balancing satellite numbers against subscriber numbers to reduce emissions per user.

Geographical differences in emissions per subscriber mainly reflect the launch vehicles assumed for each country's constellations. Russian constellations, modeled on the expendable Soyuz-FG, have the highest emissions per subscriber, at approximately 429 kg CO$_2$, compared to a mean across constellations of 286 kg. Canada and the United Kingdom have much lower emissions per subscriber, associated with Lightspeed and OneWeb launching on Falcon 9. Launcher transportation is a minor contributor for every rocket, so the sail-assisted \textit{Canop\'ee} vessel used for Ariane 6, which reduces shipping emissions by approximately 30\%, has a small effect on total emissions.

The rapid growth of satellite megaconstellations poses significant long-term environmental risks. Modeling suggests that an annual cadence of 1,000 hydrocarbon rocket launches could produce radiative forcing comparable to that of global subsonic aviation \citep{boley_satellite_2021}. Additionally, the injection of particulates like alumina and black carbon into the stratosphere could exacerbate radiative forcing and ozone depletion. These risks underscore the need for sustainable practices to ensure that the benefits of satellite constellations do not come at an unsustainable environmental cost.

Technological innovation offers promising solutions to these challenges. Exploiting natural orbital perturbations for station-keeping could reduce satellite propellant budgets by up to about 60\% \citep{singh_low_2020}, while integrating eco-friendly propulsion systems, such as ion or electric propulsion, can minimize emissions during satellite station-keeping and orbital adjustments. Modular satellite architectures that extend operational lifespans and reduce the frequency of replacement launches represent another effective strategy for mitigating emissions.

The findings of this study emphasize the importance of adopting a holistic approach to sustainability in the satellite industry. Filling launches closer to capacity, prioritizing rockets with lower emissions per tonne of payload, and expanding reuse are the most direct steps for reducing the emissions of satellite deployment. International collaboration will be critical in establishing global standards for sustainable satellite deployment, ensuring alignment with global climate goals.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec7}

This study provides a life cycle inventory of the CO$_2$ emissions associated with deploying satellite megaconstellations, covering launcher production, transportation, propellant combustion and satellite Kr/Xe propellant production.

This research contributes to the space sustainability field by identifying the primary drivers of these emissions, propellant combustion and launcher production. Additionally, it introduces the ORACLE project, an open-source model with the datasets and visualization tools needed to reproduce and extend every result.

Our analysis reveals that launch events and launcher production dominate total emissions, collectively accounting for 92.5\% of the total. The average emissions per rocket launch are approximately 1.23 kt CO$_2$, equivalent to the annual carbon absorption of 56,000 trees. Reuse lowers production emissions per launch by 81--97\% relative to building a new vehicle for every flight, and production accounts for only 14.1\% of Falcon 9's per-launch emissions compared to 45.9\% across all rockets analyzed. Fully deploying the 24 constellations would emit about 9.1 Mt CO$_2$. Emissions per subscriber average 286 kg CO$_2$ across constellations, ranging from 4 kg to 1,163 kg, and the number of satellites carried per launch is the assumption with the largest effect on the results.

This study has limitations. Assumptions about the number of satellites per rocket, stand-in launch vehicles, material-specific emission factors, the Kr/Xe emission factor, and reusability parameters may not fully capture the diversity of satellite constellations and launch systems. Satellite manufacturing beyond Kr/Xe propellant, launcher fabrication electricity and non-CO$_2$ effects are not included. Future research should refine these parameters, incorporate real-world operational data, and explore emerging technologies such as kinetic launch systems and alternative propulsion methods. Further investigation into the cumulative effects of launches on atmospheric chemistry and orbital debris is also necessary to ensure the long-term sustainability of satellite constellations.

A key contribution of this study is its inclusion of a broader range of international launch vehicles in environmental analyses. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the global impact of satellite constellations, addressing a significant gap in previous research. By expanding the scope of analysis beyond U.S. and European launch vehicles to include rockets developed by other nations, this study offers a more accurate representation of the emissions profile associated with global satellite deployment.

Balancing the transformative benefits of satellite megaconstellations, such as global connectivity and advancements in communication technologies, with their environmental impacts is critical. Strategies like choosing launchers with lower emissions per tonne of payload, filling each launch closer to capacity, and adopting lower-carbon propellants can reduce emissions. Modular satellite designs that extend operational lifespans and reduce replacement frequency also offer a viable pathway to sustainability.

International collaboration and policy development will further play a vital role in promoting sustainable growth within the space industry. Establishing global standards for satellite deployment, encouraging reusable launch systems, and incentivizing eco-friendly practices will ensure that the expansion of satellite constellations aligns with global sustainability goals.

In conclusion, this study highlights the need to integrate environmental sustainability into the design, development, and deployment of satellite megaconstellations. By addressing key areas such as reusability, emissions efficiency, and innovative technologies, the space industry can balance technological progress with environmental stewardship, paving the way for a sustainable future in satellite deployment and space exploration.
